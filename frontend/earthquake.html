    The Shaking City

    "Another boring Tuesday," Kenji muttered, slouching in his chair. The afternoon sun streamed through the classroom window, illuminating dust motes dancing in the air. Across from him, his friend Akari was sketching furiously in her notebook, completely absorbed.

    1

    Suddenly, a low rumble started, and the classroom began to shake. Pencils rolled off desks, and the windows rattled in their frames. "Whoa, what's happening?" Kenji yelled, grabbing the sides of his desk.

    2

    "It's an earthquake! Get under your desk!" Akari shouted, already diving for cover. She pulled Kenji down with her, just as a bookshelf at the front of the room toppled over with a crash.

    3

    The shaking stopped as quickly as it had begun. A nervous silence filled the room. Their teacher, Mr. Tanaka, moved through the classroom. "Is everyone okay? Stay under your desks for a moment, just in case there's an aftershock."

    4

    Later that day, Mr. Tanaka dedicated the class to understanding earthquakes. "Our planet's surface is made of huge pieces, like a puzzle, called tectonic plates," he explained, drawing on the whiteboard. "These plates are always moving, but usually very slowly."

    5

    "Sometimes," he continued, "the edges of these plates get stuck. Pressure builds up, and when they finally slip, a huge amount of energy is released in waves. That's the shaking we feel." He pointed to a spot on his drawing. "The place where they slip is called a fault line."

    6

    "There are two main types of waves," Mr. Tanaka added. "P-waves, or primary waves, are the fastest. They're the first jolt you feel. S-waves, or secondary waves, are slower and move side-to-side. They cause the more violent shaking and most of the damage."

    7

    "We measure an earthquake's energy with the Richter scale, which measures magnitude. But the shaking you actually experience is called intensity, measured on the Mercalli scale. A 6.0 magnitude quake can feel very different depending on how close you are to the epicenter."

    8

    Akari raised her hand. "Does this happen a lot here?" Mr. Tanaka nodded grimly. "Our city is built near a major fault line. History tells us that large quakes have happened before and will happen again. That’s why being prepared isn’t just a good idea—it's essential."

    9

    After school, the conversation continued. "That was really scary," Akari said to Kenji. "It's not about worrying, it's about being ready. My family has an emergency kit, but I want to make sure it's up to date. We should make a plan!"

    10

    When Kenji got home, he brought it up with his parents. "An earthquake kit? Don't be so dramatic, Kenji," his dad said, not looking up from his newspaper. "That was a little tremor. Lightning doesn't strike twice."

    11

    Feeling uneasy, Kenji went to his room and opened his laptop. He searched for the city's disaster preparedness website. He found checklists, guides, and videos. The more he read, the more he realized Mr. Tanaka and Akari were right.

    12

    The next day, Kenji went to Akari's house. "You're right," he said, showing her the checklists on his phone. "My parents think I'm overreacting, but I want to be ready. Can you help me build a kit?" Akari smiled. "Of course. Let's start now."

    13

    At Akari's house, her parents helped them. "First, water," said her mom, holding up a large bottle. "At least one gallon per person, per day, for three days. And non-perishable food—don't forget a manual can opener!"

    14

    "Next, safety items," added Akari's dad. He showed them a flashlight with extra batteries, a first-aid kit, a whistle, and dust masks. "A flashlight in case the power is out, and a whistle to signal for help if you get trapped. The masks help with dust from debris."

    15

    They continued packing. "Copies of important documents," Akari said, placing a waterproof folder into a backpack. "And cash," Kenji added, remembering what he read. "ATMs and credit card machines won't work if the power is out."

    16

    Akari's mom unfolded a map. "We have a family communication plan. We have a meeting place right outside, and another one at the park. We also have an out-of-state contact who everyone calls to check in with, since local lines might be jammed."

    17

    "And see how our big bookshelf is bolted to the wall?" Akari's dad said, tapping the tall piece of furniture. "Securing heavy furniture prevents it from falling and blocking an exit." Kenji looked around his own house in his mind, realizing nothing was secured.

    18

    A week later, Kenji was at home when the ground began to shake again. A deep, violent roar echoed from the earth. The lights flickered and went out, plunging the room into darkness. His bookshelf crashed to the floor. This was the big one.

    19

    But Kenji knew exactly what to do. He didn't panic. He immediately dropped to the floor, took cover under his sturdy wooden desk, and held on tight to the legs. "Drop, Cover, and Hold On," he whispered, the words a calming mantra.

    20

    The shaking seemed to last forever. When it finally stopped, Kenji counted to sixty before cautiously crawling out. His room was a disaster zone, but he was safe.

    21

    He grabbed his new emergency backpack from by the door. He tried to call his parents, but the lines were dead. He remembered the plan and sent a quick text to Akari: "U ok? Heading to park." A moment later, a reply buzzed through: "Ok. U? See you there."

    22

    Kenji made his way to the designated park in his neighborhood. He saw neighbors helping each other, checking for gas leaks, and sharing information. There was damage, but there was also calm and cooperation.

    23

    He found Akari with her family at the park. They were both relieved to see each other safe. They gave each other a quick, grateful hug.

    24

    "I'm so glad we were prepared," Kenji said, looking around at the organized response. "You were right, Akari. It's not about being scared, it's about being smart and having a plan." Akari smiled, knowing they had done the right thing. They were safe, and they were ready.
